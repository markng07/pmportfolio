Growing Pay Later Profit
While Controlling Bad Debt

A deep-dive into a high-growth BNPL product's profit divergence problem — tracing hidden bad debt back to a single merchant segment through multi-system SQL analysis.

01 · Situation

Strong Growth on the Surface — But Something Was Off

The BNPL product I managed was one of the fastest-growing products in the company. By mid-2023, it had surpassed active users, growing roughly month-over-month. Revenue was climbing. Transaction volume was hitting new highs every quarter. From the outside, it looked like a success story.

But when I looked at the profit trend, something didn't add up. Revenue had grown 3.1× over the year. Profit had grown only 0.8×. They were supposed to move together — and they weren't.

The Head of Business interpreted this as a signal to double down: more features, more marketing spend. I disagreed. Before pouring fuel on the fire, I needed to understand what was already burning.

The Core Question

While users and revenue grew 3× in 12 months, why did operating profit grow less than 1×? Which cost line was scaling out of control — and why?

02 · Data & Approach

Six Systems, 50 Million Rows, One Flat Model

The challenge wasn't a lack of data — it was that the data lived across six siloed systems: the transaction ledger, the repayment engine, the collections platform, the merchant database, the user profile store, and the credit scoring system. No single table told the full story.

My analytical strategy had two rules. First, aggregate before joining — pre-summarize each system into monthly roll-ups so I wasn't performing multi-million-row Cartesian joins. Second, build a cohort model — every user tagged to their approval month, so I could track credit behavior over time rather than reading a snapshot that masked deterioration in newer cohorts.

03 · SQL Analysis

The Queries That Found the Signal

I structured the analysis in six sequential steps, each building on the last. The goal was to move from what is happening (P&L waterfall) to where it's happening (merchant segmentation) to why it's happening (cash-out fraud pattern).

Step 1 — Pre-aggregate each source system

Before any join, each table is summarized to monthly grain per user. This reduces the transaction table from 52M rows to ~1.4M — a 97% reduction — before touching any other system.

step1_pre_aggregate.sql
Performance 
-- Aggregate transactions: 52M rows → ~1.4M
CREATE TEMP TABLE txn_summary AS
SELECT
    user_id,
    merchant_id,
    DATE_TRUNC('month', txn_date)   AS txn_month,
    COUNT(*)                         AS txn_count,
    SUM(txn_amount)                  AS total_txn_amount,
    SUM(fee_revenue)                 AS total_fee_revenue,
    SUM(interest_revenue)            AS total_interest_revenue
FROM transactions
GROUP BY 1, 2, 3;

-- Aggregate repayments: 31M rows → ~820K
CREATE TEMP TABLE repayment_summary AS
SELECT
    user_id,
    DATE_TRUNC('month', repayment_date) AS repayment_month,
    SUM(amount_repaid)                   AS total_repaid,
    SUM(amount_due)                      AS total_due,
    SUM(amount_due - amount_repaid)      AS total_outstanding
FROM repayments
GROUP BY 1, 2;

-- Aggregate bad debt write-offs: 4.8M rows → ~190K
CREATE TEMP TABLE bad_debt_summary AS
SELECT
    user_id,
    DATE_TRUNC('month', writeoff_date) AS writeoff_month,
    SUM(writeoff_amount)                AS total_bad_debt
FROM collections_writeoffs
GROUP BY 1, 2;

Step 2 — Build the master cohort table

Join the pre-aggregated summaries into a single flat model. Every row represents one user, one merchant, one month — with all revenue and cost metrics attached. Crucially, cohort_month tags each user to their acquisition period, enabling vintage analysis later.

step2_master_cohort.sql
Core Model 
CREATE TEMP TABLE master_cohort AS
SELECT
    u.user_id,
    DATE_TRUNC('month', u.approval_date)  AS cohort_month,
    u.acquisition_channel,
    u.credit_tier,
    t.txn_month,
    t.merchant_id,
    m.merchant_category,
    m.merchant_type,                      -- 'mom_and_pop' | 'chain' | 'ecommerce'
    t.txn_count,
    t.total_txn_amount,
    t.total_fee_revenue,
    t.total_interest_revenue,
    COALESCE(r.total_repaid,   0)           AS total_repaid,
    COALESCE(r.total_due,     0)           AS total_due,
    COALESCE(b.total_bad_debt, 0)          AS total_bad_debt
FROM users u
JOIN txn_summary t         ON u.user_id = t.user_id
JOIN merchant_summary m   ON t.merchant_id = m.merchant_id
LEFT JOIN repayment_summary r
    ON u.user_id = r.user_id
   AND t.txn_month = r.repayment_month
LEFT JOIN bad_debt_summary b
    ON u.user_id = b.user_id
   AND t.txn_month = b.writeoff_month;

Step 3 — P&L waterfall: index cost lines to revenue growth

This query answered the first question: which cost line is growing disproportionately? By computing a MoM growth rate for both revenue and bad debt simultaneously, the divergence became immediately visible in the output.

step3_pnl_waterfall.sql
Diagnostic 
SELECT
    txn_month,
    SUM(total_fee_revenue + total_interest_revenue)  AS total_revenue,
    SUM(total_bad_debt)                               AS total_bad_debt,

    -- Bad debt as % of revenue (the key ratio)
    ROUND(SUM(total_bad_debt) /
        NULLIF(SUM(total_fee_revenue + total_interest_revenue), 0) * 100, 1)
                                                          AS bad_debt_pct_revenue,

    -- MoM growth comparison
    ROUND((SUM(total_bad_debt) -
        LAG(SUM(total_bad_debt)) OVER (ORDER BY txn_month))
        / NULLIF(LAG(SUM(total_bad_debt)) OVER (ORDER BY txn_month), 0) * 100, 1)
                                                          AS bad_debt_mom_pct,

    ROUND((SUM(total_fee_revenue + total_interest_revenue) -
        LAG(SUM(total_fee_revenue + total_interest_revenue))
            OVER (ORDER BY txn_month))
        / NULLIF(LAG(SUM(total_fee_revenue + total_interest_revenue))
            OVER (ORDER BY txn_month), 0) * 100, 1)
                                                          AS revenue_mom_pct
FROM master_cohort
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;

Step 4 — Cohort credit quality: are newer cohorts defaulting more?

This query computes bad debt ratios by months-since-approval for each acquisition cohort. If the Q3 cohort shows a 12% bad debt ratio at month 3 while the Q1 cohort showed 4% at the same stage, the problem is at the acquisition gate — you are approving riskier users over time.

step4_cohort_quality.sql
Vintage Analysis 
SELECT
    cohort_month,
    txn_month,
    DATEDIFF('month', cohort_month, txn_month)   AS months_on_book,
    COUNT(DISTINCT user_id)                      AS active_users,
    SUM(total_bad_debt)                          AS bad_debt,
    SUM(total_fee_revenue + total_interest_revenue) AS revenue,
    ROUND(SUM(total_bad_debt) /
        NULLIF(SUM(total_fee_revenue
            + total_interest_revenue), 0) * 100, 2)
                                                 AS bad_debt_ratio_pct
FROM master_cohort
GROUP BY 1, 2, 3
ORDER BY 1, 3;

Step 5 — Merchant segmentation: where does the bad debt live?

After confirming bad debt was the culprit, I traced it along two dimensions: merchant category and merchant type. The pct_of_total_bad_debt window function was the key — it ranked every merchant segment by its share of total bad debt across the entire portfolio, not just its own bad debt ratio.

step5_merchant_segmentation.sql
Root Cause 
SELECT
    merchant_category,
    merchant_type,
    COUNT(DISTINCT user_id)                           AS unique_users,
    SUM(total_txn_amount)                             AS total_volume,
    SUM(total_fee_revenue + total_interest_revenue)   AS total_revenue,
    SUM(total_bad_debt)                               AS total_bad_debt,

    -- Bad debt rate within this segment
    ROUND(SUM(total_bad_debt) /
        NULLIF(SUM(total_fee_revenue
            + total_interest_revenue), 0) * 100, 1)
                                                      AS bad_debt_rate_pct,

    -- Share of TOTAL portfolio bad debt (window function)
    ROUND(SUM(total_bad_debt) /
        NULLIF(SUM(SUM(total_bad_debt)) OVER (), 0) * 100, 1)
                                                      AS pct_of_total_bad_debt
FROM master_cohort
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY total_bad_debt DESC;

Step 6 — Flag high-risk merchants for the scoring model

The final query operationalized the findings into a three-tier risk classification. A merchant flagged as high risk had both an abnormally high average transaction value (above the 90th percentile) and a bad debt rate above 15% — the exact behavioral signature of cash-out fraud collusion.

step6_merchant_risk_flags.sql
Actionable Output 
WITH txn_p90 AS (
    SELECT
        PERCENTILE_CONT(0.90)
            WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY total_txn_amount)  AS p90_txn_value
    FROM master_cohort
)
SELECT
    mc.merchant_id,
    mc.merchant_category,
    mc.merchant_type,
    COUNT(DISTINCT mc.user_id)                      AS unique_users,
    AVG(mc.total_txn_amount /
        NULLIF(mc.txn_count, 0))                    AS avg_txn_value,
    SUM(mc.total_bad_debt)                          AS total_bad_debt,
    ROUND(SUM(mc.total_bad_debt) /
        NULLIF(SUM(mc.total_txn_amount), 0) * 100, 1)
                                                    AS bad_debt_rate_pct,
    CASE
        WHEN SUM(mc.total_bad_debt) /
             NULLIF(SUM(mc.total_txn_amount), 0) > 0.15
         AND AVG(mc.total_txn_amount /
             NULLIF(mc.txn_count, 0)) > p90.p90_txn_value
        THEN 'HIGH RISK — Escalate'
        WHEN SUM(mc.total_bad_debt) /
             NULLIF(SUM(mc.total_txn_amount), 0) BETWEEN 0.05 AND 0.15
        THEN 'MEDIUM RISK — Monitor'
        ELSE 'LOW RISK — No Action'
    END                                             AS risk_tier
FROM master_cohort mc, txn_p90 p90
GROUP BY 1, 2, 3, p90.p90_txn_value
ORDER BY bad_debt_rate_pct DESC;

05 · Recommendations

Optimize Risk Before Investing in Growth

The analysis made one thing clear: we did not have a demand problem, a product problem, or a marketing problem. We had a risk containment problem concentrated in a specific, identifiable merchant segment. Before approving any new marketing spend, we needed to plug the leak.

My Recommendation to Leadership

Pause incremental marketing investment and deploy engineering resources to build a Merchant Risk Scoring model. This would deliver a higher expected ROI than new user acquisition, because each fraudulent merchant removed from the network immediately improved the unit economics of the existing user base.

  • 01

    Build a Merchant Risk Scoring model

    Use the flagging query (Step 6) as the foundation. Score each merchant on three signals: average transaction value percentile, bad debt rate over a rolling 60-day window, and transaction velocity (number of unique users per week). Merchants in the top 5% of combined score get auto-flagged for review.

  • 02

    Implement tiered intervention, not a binary block

    High-risk merchants receive delayed payout (72-hour settlement hold), a per-transaction cap, and mandatory manual review for transactions above a defined threshold. Medium-risk merchants receive monitoring and automated alerts. This preserves legitimate volume while eliminating the fraud pattern.

  • 03

    Re-underwrite the Jun–Aug 2023 cohorts

    Users acquired through high-risk merchant channels during the peak fraud period (Jun–Aug) should be subject to reduced credit limits until their repayment track record improves. This limits future bad debt exposure from the already-compromised cohorts without deactivating accounts entirely.

  • 04

    Only then re-enable marketing investment

    Once the bad debt ratio returns to its January baseline of ~6%, resume growth investment — but redirect channel spend away from mom-and-pop merchant partnerships and toward e-commerce and chain retail, where the bad debt rate is below 4%.

06 · Results

The Numbers After Intervention

The Data Science and Risk teams built the Merchant Risk Scoring model in four weeks. We ran a controlled pilot for three months — applying the tiered intervention model to a randomly selected 50% of flagged merchants while the other 50% served as a holdout group.

The results validated the analysis and exceeded the initial business case.

Critically, the intervention had a minimal impact on legitimate revenue. Because the tiered model used delayed payout and transaction caps rather than outright bans, genuine small merchants were largely unaffected. Less than 3% of total transaction volume was disrupted, while the bad debt ratio was cut by more than half within two months.

Once unit economics turned positive, leadership approved the renewed marketing investment. In Q1 2024, the product re-entered growth mode — this time with a risk infrastructure that could scale alongside it.

What I Would Do Differently

The fraud signal was present in the data as early as February 2023, but the monthly review cadence meant it was not escalated until May. Building an automated bad debt ratio alert — triggering when any merchant segment's bad debt rate crosses a defined threshold for two consecutive weeks — would have surfaced the issue approximately two months earlier.
