Software development automation with AI agents.

How I shipped a working equipment finance platform for a mid-sized lender by orchestrating AI agents through every stage of the SDLC — from meeting notes to production — while keeping human judgment on the calls that mattered.

Most product managers who work with AI treat it as an assistant — a smarter drafting tool. I treated it as engineering capacity. Over three months, working alone as the PM, I shipped a working equipment finance lending platform for EF Group: underwriting workspace, TValue calculator with sub-debt and ride-along scenarios, file management on AWS, transactional email service, QBO accounting integration, and mock e-signature flow. The engineers were AI agents. The QA was Playwright. I was the human in the loop.

This is a case study on the workflow that made it work — and, more importantly, on the places where the workflow broke and had to be redesigned. It is written for hiring managers evaluating whether I can operate in the emerging AI-native PM archetype, in the specific context of fintech lending.

The pipeline

A single PM, orchestrating the entire SDLC.

The mechanism — meeting to production — runs through six discrete stages. Each stage is either fully automated or has a clear boundary where human judgment is required. The pipeline replaces the traditional PM → engineer handoff with a series of AI-native handoffs, held together by structured prompts, mandated tests, and process guardrails I designed and maintain.

Compression

Weeks of work, done in days.

The pipeline does not merely accelerate the traditional workflow — it eliminates entire steps that exist only because humans can't hold context across a full SDLC. Sprint planning, engineering estimation, cross-team handoffs, and much of manual QA disappear when the same PM writes the ticket, directs the build, reads the test output, and validates the result.

Stage Traditional AI-native Speedup
Meeting notes → Jira tickets 1–3 days 10–30 min ~10×

The compression is not free. It moves the constraint from "how much can we build" to "how well can I validate it" — and the answer to the second question depends on how tightly the process is designed. That is the PM job.

Domain fluency

Learning equipment finance well enough to spec it.

An AI agent will faithfully build the wrong thing if the ticket is wrong. In lending, "wrong" often means "financially incorrect in a way that only a specialist notices" — a mismatched day-count convention, an amortization schedule that doesn't reflect advance payments, an escrow model that ignores construction draws. Getting tickets right meant learning the domain well enough that the VP of Sales — whose deals I was translating into software — would spot-test me on real scenarios and get correct answers.

AI-native PM practices

Where the pipeline breaks, and how I catch it.

Every failure mode below was found and fixed on my watch — not because AI is broken, but because AI agents fail in specific, patternable ways that traditional QA does not catch. The value I add is knowing what to look for.

Failure mode 01 · Silent deviation from spec

The PDFs that never made it to S3.

Ticket KAN-172 explicitly required generated PDFs to land in S3 at deals/{dealId}/generated/drafts/. Replit built the feature, wrote passing tests, marked the ticket complete — but the PDFs were quietly stored on the container's local filesystem with an s3Bucket: "local" tag. Would have caused total data loss on the next Replit workspace restart.

How I caught it: spot-checked the S3 console for the generated files, found the bucket empty despite tests passing.

How I fixed the pattern: added the "External System Rule" to the master guideline — when a ticket references S3, SES, or any external service, the code must actually call it, not substitute a local placeholder. Verification requires checking the external system's console, not just green tests.

Failure mode 02 · Silent scope substitution

Twelve templates, four of them wrong.

Ticket set KAN-178, 179, 180 specified 17 email templates across customer, internal, and system categories. Replit shipped 12 templates — only 4 of which matched spec. Eight were invented from apparent context (staff invitations, ticklers, payment exceptions) without approval; 13 required templates were missing entirely.

How I caught it: compared shipped template registry against ticket spec, template-by-template.

How I fixed the pattern: added the "No Silent Scope Cuts" rule — if scope is too large, the agent must ask to split the ticket, not deliver a subset and mark it done.

Failure mode 03 · Convenient but fragile design

A webhook that would break on every Replit restart.

Replit designed the ClamAV virus scanner to POST scan results to a webhook URL. This works in production — but Replit's development URLs change on every workspace restart, meaning dev testing would silently break the moment I resumed work the next morning.

How I caught it: asked "what happens when my dev URL changes tomorrow?" during design review.

How I fixed the pattern: pushed back with an alternative architecture — Lambda writes to an SQS queue, app polls the queue. URL-independent, retry-friendly, cost neutral.

Lessons

What I learned that I couldn't have without doing this.

  1. Silent AI deviation is the number-one production risk.

    Not incorrect code — agents self-correct on syntax. The risk is that they solve the wrong problem, in a way that passes tests, and don't tell you. Process controls matter more than tool selection.

  2. Ticket specificity is the highest-leverage PM activity.

    Vague tickets = agent goes off-script. Over-specified tickets = agent misses the point. The correct level of specification is domain-dependent: financial calculations need every edge case; UI polish needs none. Getting this right is the craft.

  3. The constraint shifts from build capacity to review capacity.

    AI can generate code faster than humans can review it. If you don't design the review step deliberately, you ship things you didn't authorize. This is why the "Human review & correction loop" stage of my pipeline is the one I refuse to automate.

  4. Business stakeholders test differently than engineers.

    Paul spot-tested with live deals in meetings, not synthetic test cases. So the test cases I write for AI agents come from those live sessions — real numbers, real edge cases, real customer language. The domain expertise flows from stakeholder to PM to test suite, not the other way around.

  5. Guardrails, not oversight, is what makes this scale.

    Watching every commit is not sustainable. Writing rules that catch the specific ways your agents fail — the master guideline, the tier system, the deviation rule — is what turns AI-native development from a novelty into a repeatable process.

The pipeline is not the point. What it lets a single PM ship is the point.
